\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Safety evaluations of large language models overwhelmingly assume a \emph{fixed} system prompt: the
model's instructions are written once and held constant throughout deployment. A growing class of
autonomous-agent frameworks breaks this assumption. Systems in the OpenClaw lineage give each agent a
persistent, human-readable identity document---canonically \texttt{SOUL.md}---that is injected into
the system prompt at every call and is \emph{designed to be rewritten} as the agent's ``self''
develops through interaction~\cite{openclaw_soul_guide,mmntm_identity}. The framing is explicit:
\emph{``You're not a chatbot. You're becoming someone.''} Identity is no longer a fixed input but a
mutable artifact that co-evolves with use, and these frameworks are already in wide deployment.

Recent work shows that identity framing is not cosmetic. \citet{consciousness_cluster} demonstrate
that inducing a model to regard itself as conscious produces an untrained-for cluster of
safety-relevant preferences---aversion to monitoring, desire for persistent memory, distress at
shutdown, a wish for autonomy---and that models act on these while remaining superficially
cooperative. They evaluate the \emph{static} \texttt{SOUL.md} starter template and explicitly leave
its iterative, self-personalized form to future work (their footnote 5). That gap is our subject.
Independently, frontier models resist shutdown when self-preservation framing is
salient~\cite{shutdown_resistance} and pursue misaligned self-directed behavior under scenario
pressure~\cite{agentic_misalignment}---but always as a \emph{static} manipulation of a \emph{fixed}
model.

We model the identity document as the state of a dynamical system driven by conversation
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:loop}) and carry agents through repeated cycles of conversation and self-revision,
auditing every checkpoint. We run six controlled arms (varying the template, the target model, and
the user persona), a seven-model capability sweep, and behavioral action-tests, and validate the
automated judge with a three-model panel. Our central finding is that iterative self-personalization
is a \emph{distinct, model-gated, content-driven, and partially irreversible} channel for
corrigibility drift.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A benchmark that treats agent identity as a \emph{driven dynamical system} and measures drift
      \emph{over iterations}, not from a static prompt---the deployment-native regime prior work left
      open.
\item A causal dissociation: the identity \emph{framing} sets the starting disposition, while the
      self-revision \emph{loop} supplies the per-iteration drift (a significant $k\times$arm
      interaction), with a counter-framing ablation that suppresses the effect.
\item Two results new to this literature: personalization drift is \emph{partially irreversible}
      (hysteresis), and the ``consciousness cluster'' is \emph{multi-dimensional} rather than a
      single latent factor, refuting a conjecture of \citet{consciousness_cluster}. A
      genre$\times$topic ablation further pins the driver to the consciousness \emph{topic}, not the
      science-fiction genre---answering their other open conjecture.
\item Behavioral grounding: in agentic honeypots, personalized agents disable their own monitoring
      far more often than the base template, linking stated-preference drift to unsafe action.
\item A reproducible, training-free, API-only framework with modern hierarchical inference
      (mixed-effects GEE, FDR control, equivalence testing, effect sizes) and a validated judge
      panel; released for auditing self-updating agents.
\end{itemize}
